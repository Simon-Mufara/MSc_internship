Key Experimental Design Gaps & Platform Responses
🔬
1. Tissue Dissociation Protocol — Direct Impact on QC Thresholds
Enzymatic dissociation (collagenase, trypsin) versus mechanical dissociation produces systematically different stress response gene profiles. Enzymatic protocols induce heat-shock and immediate early gene expression (HSP70, JUN, FOS) that are biologically artifactual. This affects which genes appear in the HVG selection and which clusters you identify.

Platform response: The platform's Stage 2 QC now prompts users to declare their dissociation method. Enzymatic dissociation triggers a stress gene exclusion module, and the HVG selection in Stage 3 filters known dissociation-induced artefact genes before clustering.
→ Directly changes which cells survive QC and which clusters emerge in Stage 6 (Leiden). A bioinformatician who ignores this will cluster stress-response artefacts as real biological populations.
📦
2. Batch Effects from Library Preparation — The Silent Confounder
Library preparation batches run on different days, by different operators, with different reagent lots introduce systematic technical variation that is indistinguishable from biological signal without prior knowledge of the experimental design. A bioinformatician receiving data without batch metadata will confound technical with biological variation.

Platform response: The Stage 1 upload interface now requires batch metadata declaration. Multi-batch datasets are routed through Harmony or scVI-based batch correction before neighbourhood graph construction, with a pre/post-correction embedding comparison shown to the user.
→ Without this, UMAP clusters in Stage 7 may reflect which sample was run on which machine, not which cell type it is. Wetlab knowledge of experimental batches is prerequisite data, not optional metadata.
🧬
3. Biological vs Technical Replicates — What Your Statistics Actually Test
A bioinformatician receiving three samples from the same patient (technical replicates) and three samples from three patients (biological replicates) may treat them identically. They are not. Differential expression analysis with technical replicates pseudoreplicates — inflating statistical power and producing false positives. The H3ABioNet training materials frequently under-address this distinction.

Platform response: The Stage 8 DE analysis module now requires users to declare replicate structure. Pseudobulk methods (DESeq2 via decoupleR) are applied for multi-donor designs; within-sample DE is restricted appropriately. The report section explicitly states the statistical model used and its assumptions.
→ This is publishability-critical. Reviewers at Nature Methods and Genome Biology will reject papers where DE analysis uses inappropriate replicate structures. The platform enforcing this makes it a quality-control gatekeeping tool, not just an analysis convenience.
❄️
4. Sample Preservation & RNA Quality — What RIN Scores Tell You
Fresh vs cryopreserved samples, FFPE sections, and fresh-frozen tissue produce systematically different RNA integrity profiles. A cryopreserved PBMC sample that experienced a partial freeze-thaw cycle will show elevated mitochondrial read fractions and depleted ribosomal RNA — patterns that standard QC interprets as dying cells, triggering aggressive filtering that removes real biological signal. The standard 20% mitochondrial threshold is calibrated for fresh PBMC and will over-filter cryopreserved samples.

Platform response: Stage 1 now includes a sample condition declaration (fresh / cryopreserved / FFPE / frozen). Mitochondrial thresholds in Stage 2 are adaptively adjusted: cryopreserved samples use a relaxed threshold (30–35%), with a flagged warning that the researcher should validate against RIN score metadata. This is the adaptive parameter system described in Phase 3 AI — but implementable now with rule-based logic from wetlab knowledge.
→ At UFS-NGS Unit, rotavirus stool samples and respiratory clinical specimens have variable preservation quality. This is directly applicable to the metagenomics pipeline you're building there.
🔢
5. Cell Loading Density — Doublet Rate Is Experimental, Not Computational
Doublet rates in 10x Chromium data are a direct function of how many cells were loaded into the microfluidic chip. The standard recommendation is 6,000–10,000 cells per lane; over-loading a chip to maximise throughput increases doublet rates from ~4% to >10%. DoubletFinder and Scrublet can detect doublets computationally, but their performance degrades when doublet rates exceed ~8%. A bioinformatician who doesn't know the cell loading density cannot appropriately parameterise doublet detection.

Platform response: Stage 1 now requests cells-loaded value. The expected doublet rate is calculated from the manufacturer's loading curve and used to parameterise Scrublet's expected doublet rate parameter — replacing the default assumption with wet-lab knowledge.
→ This is the clearest example of wetlab-computational integration. The wet-lab decision (how many cells to load) directly determines the optimal computational parameter (expected doublet rate). The platform now closes that loop.
⏱️
6. Sequencing Depth — Reads per Cell Is a Wet-Lab Budget Decision
The number of reads per cell (sequencing depth) is determined when the library is submitted for sequencing, not during analysis. Low-depth libraries (1,000–3,000 reads/cell) produce sparse count matrices where rare transcripts are frequently missed — leading to poor clustering resolution and unreliable marker gene identification. High-depth libraries are expensive. This trade-off is made in the wet lab, but its consequences appear computationally.

Platform response: Stage 1 includes a sequencing depth declaration (reads per cell). The platform calculates median genes detected and flags datasets where depth is insufficient for the declared analysis goal (rare cell type identification requires deeper sequencing than broad cell type annotation). This guidance appears before QC begins, not after the analysis fails.
→ Applicable directly to UFS-NGS rotavirus WGS: sequencing depth decisions for variant calling are made at library preparation. Your platform can flag depth-quality issues before downstream analysis begins.
